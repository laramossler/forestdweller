A windstorm last winter brought down a large Douglas fir on the property line. Rather than let it rot or pay to have it hauled away, we decided to mill it on-site and use the timber for building projects.

## The Design

The tool shelter needed to be:
- Large enough for chainsaws, tools, and forest management equipment
- Open on one side for easy access
- Simple enough to build with hand tools
- Strong enough to last decades

We settled on a traditional timber frame design: mortise and tenon joints, wooden pegs, no metal fasteners in the primary structure.

## Milling the Timber

Using an Alaskan chainsaw mill, we processed the downed fir into dimensional lumber:
- 6x6 posts for the corner supports
- 4x6 beams for the horizontal members
- 2x6 boards for roof decking

The milling process was slow—a full day to process one log—but the cost savings and quality of the timber made it worthwhile.

## The Build

Traditional timber framing requires precision. Each mortise and tenon joint needs to fit tightly, and the entire frame needs to be square and plumb.

We worked slowly, measuring twice and cutting once. The joints were cut by hand with chisels and a Japanese saw. Each connection was pegged with oak dowels.

## Lessons Learned

- Green Douglas fir is significantly easier to work than dried lumber
- Traditional joinery is slower but more satisfying than modern construction
- Working with local materials creates a different relationship to the land
- A hand-built structure carries the marks of its maker—this is a feature, not a bug

The shelter is complete. It will house tools for the restoration work and stand as a reminder that building with care and traditional methods is still possible.
