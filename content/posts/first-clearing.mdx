The English ivy had been growing unchecked for at least 30 years. It covered the forest floor in a dense mat, climbed Douglas firs to heights of 60 feet, and effectively prevented any native plants from establishing.

## The Scale of the Problem

Walking through this section of the property for the first time was sobering. What should have been diverse forest understory—sword ferns, Oregon grape, salal, trillium—was instead a monoculture of invasive ivy. The weight of the ivy on the trees was creating structural problems, and several smaller trees had already been pulled down.

## The Approach

We decided to start with the trees first. Cutting the ivy vines at the base allows the climbing portion to die back over several months, making it safer to work around the trees and reducing the immediate structural threat.

The ground ivy would be a longer project—systematic removal, section by section, over multiple seasons.

## What We Learned

- Cut ivy vines need to be cut twice: once at ground level, and again at shoulder height, to prevent re-sprouting
- The dying ivy provides incredible habitat for insects and fungi during decomposition
- Native plants begin returning almost immediately once the ivy mat is removed
- This is multi-year work. Ivy removal is not a one-time event but an ongoing commitment

## Next Steps

With the first section cleared, we're watching what returns naturally before deciding what to plant. The land knows what belongs here better than we do.
